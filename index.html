Research

My work spans multi-turn neural retrieval, efficient language models, and risk-controlled prediction for clinical language and knowledge graphs.

Retrieval that holds up across a conversation

A follow-up question only makes sense in light of what came before. My SemEval 2026 system rewrites each turn into a standalone query, retrieves with a sparse model (SPLADE) and a dense model (Voyage-3-large), merges the two rankings with reciprocal rank fusion, and conditions the answer on whether the question can be answered from the evidence.

Query rewriting improved nDCG@10 by 16.3% relative to the same hybrid without it. The system placed 4th of 38 teams on retrieval and the paper received the Best System Paper Award.

Smaller models that keep their quality

At MBZUAI I co-led a WMT 2026 study of compressing Gemma 3 12B for Czech to German translation. I owned the task-calibrated 4-bit GPTQ workstream, which retained 99.7% of the dense model’s COMET score on a 300-sentence development set while cutting GPU memory from 26.6 to 17.6 GiB.

We also tested activation-aware low-rank compression, which removed 46.6% of the parameters. Combining it with quantization scored below either method alone, and the paper reports that negative result.

Paper and details

Risk-controlled phenotype normalization

Clinical language systems must link descriptions of patient abnormalities to the Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO). When the most specific prediction is unreliable, can a system return a valid, more general ancestor or abstain while keeping its error rate below a chosen target?

In a manuscript currently under review, we study hierarchical back-off over the HPO's multi-inheritance graph. We compare learned decisions with simpler hierarchical climbing rules and use finite-sample Clopper–Pearson bounds to certify candidate policies under explicit sampling assumptions.

On the standard retrospective PhenoNormBench evaluation, our back-off policy reduces observed error among answered mentions from 17.2% to 0.85% while answering 70.9% of mentions. Under dependence-aware splits, certification becomes substantially more conservative, exposing a real trade-off between reliability, coverage, and specificity.

Risk-controlled hierarchical back-off for phenotype normalization

Manuscript under peer review. Not yet accepted or published.

We study when clinical phenotype mentions can be mapped to exact Human Phenotype Ontology concepts, backed off to valid ancestors, or left unanswered under a finite-sample error-risk constraint. The comparison includes learned back-off, flat abstention, and tree/DAG hierarchical climbing. On the retrospective benchmark, learned back-off answers 70.9% of mentions at 0.85% observed error among answered cases, versus 17.2% error for the unfiltered Top-1 baseline. Dependence-aware experiments expose the cost of certification: less coverage or less informative answers. These retrospective results should not be interpreted as a prospective clinical guarantee.
